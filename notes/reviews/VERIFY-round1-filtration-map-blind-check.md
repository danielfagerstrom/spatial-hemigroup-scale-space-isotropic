## 1. The map and its normalisation, from the paper's definitions

Let (0, k_d) be an isotropic origin datum (Definition `def:isotropic-origin-datum`): k_d ≥ 0
nonincreasing on (0,∞), ∫_0^1 k_d(r) r dr < ∞, ∫_1^∞ k_d(r) dr/r < ∞. Its Lévy measure has the
polar form (3.2), so its exponent (3.3) is, after the integration over the sphere,

F(ρ) = ∫_0^∞ (1 − Ω_d(ρr)) k_d(r) dr/r,  ρ = |ξ|.

Let ϖ be the Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure −dk_d on (0,∞). The second integrability condition forces
k_d(∞) = 0, so k_d(r) = ϖ((r,∞)) at every continuity point of k_d, that is off a countable set, and
ϖ((0,∞)) = k_d(0+) =: c. Suppose 0 < c < ∞ and put σ := ϖ/c, a probability measure on (0,∞). Since
1 − Ω_d ≥ 0, Tonelli's theorem applies twice:

F(ρ) = ∫_{(0,∞)} ϖ(ds) ∫_0^s (1 − Ω_d(ρr)) dr/r
     = ∫_{(0,∞)} ϖ(ds) ∫_0^ρ (1 − Ω_d(vs)) dv/v      (r = vs/ρ)
     = c ∫_0^ρ (1 − φ(v)) dv/v,  φ(v) := ∫_{(0,∞)} Ω_d(vs) σ(ds).

φ(|ξ|) is the characteristic function of R·U, with U uniform on S^{d−1} and R independent of law
σ = −dk_d/c. So the answer to the first question of the brief is yes, with this dictionary:

| on the profile | on the law of R |
|---|---|
| k_d nonincreasing, k_d(∞) = 0, k_d(0+) = c ∈ (0,∞) | σ a probability measure on (0,∞): no mass at 0, none at ∞ |
| k_d(r) = c·σ((r,∞)) for a.e. r | the tail of σ |
| ∫_1^∞ k_d(r) dr/r < ∞ | ∫ log⁺s σ(ds) < ∞ (the two integrals are equal up to the factor c, by Tonelli) |
| ∫_0^1 k_d(r) r dr < ∞ | automatic: it is at most c/2 |
| a jump of k_d at τ | an atom of σ at τ |

*Atoms, right-continuity, "almost everywhere".* A datum's profile is determined up to a Lebesgue
null set (Proposition 3.10(1)). Two nonincreasing versions agree at their common continuity
points, so they have the same right limit at 0 and the same Stieltjes measure. The pair (c, σ) is
therefore a function of the class of k_d, and the right-continuous representative is
k_d = c·σ((·,∞)). An atom of σ at 0 would not appear in F at all: φ = p + (1−p)φ₀ gives
c(1 − φ) = c(1−p)(1 − φ₀). The condition "no atom at 0" is what makes (c, φ) a function of F.

*The logarithmic moment, read on φ.* ∫_0^1 (1 − φ(u)) du/u = ∫σ(ds) H_d(s) with
H_d(s) = ∫_0^s (1 − Ω_d(v)) dv/v, which is ~ s²/(4d) at 0 and ~ log s at ∞. So for the transform φ
of any rotation-invariant law, F_φ is finite exactly when the law has a finite logarithmic moment.
This is a property of φ alone and does not depend on the dimension in which φ is read.

Checked numerically (block A): the Lévy form and the map agree to 14 digits for the step profile
(σ = δ_τ) and the exponential profile (σ exponential, φ in closed form) in d = 1, 2, 3, 4; Ω_d
against the sphere mean; the two logarithmic integrals.

## 2. (i) and (ii)

**(i) HOLDS.** Read at the page images of Sato pp. 106, 108, 109. Lemma 17.1 (p. 106):
ψ(z) = log P̂_{Z_1}(z) for the Lévy process {Z_t} generated by (G, ρ, β). Theorem 17.5 (pp. 108–109),
verbatim in its formulas: *"Fix c > 0. (i) If ρ satisfies (17.11) ∫_{|x|>2} log|x| ρ(dx) < ∞, the
process of Ornstein–Uhlenbeck type on ℝ^d generated by (G, ρ, β, c) has a limit distribution μ with
(17.12) μ̂(z) = exp[∫_0^∞ ψ(e^{−cs}z) ds]. The distribution μ is selfdecomposable and the generating
triplet (A, ν, γ) of μ is given by (17.13) A = (1/2c)G, (17.14) ν(B) = (1/c)∫_{ℝ^d} ρ(dy) ∫_0^∞
1_B(e^{−s}y) ds, (17.15) γ = (1/c)β + (1/c)∫_{|y|>1} (y/|y|) ρ(dy). (ii) For any selfdecomposable
distribution μ on ℝ^d, there exists a unique triplet (G, ρ, β) satisfying (17.11) such that μ is
the limit distribution of the process of Ornstein–Uhlenbeck type generated by (G, ρ, β, c). Using λ
and k_ξ(r) in the expression (15.8) for the Lévy measure ν of μ in Theorem 15.10, we have (17.16)
ρ(B) = −c∫_S λ(dξ) ∫_0^∞ 1_B(rξ) dk_ξ(r)."* The record's transcription of (17.11), (17.12) and
(17.16) is exact.

With Sato's c = 1, λ the normalised surface measure and k_ξ = k_d, (17.16) is ρ = (uniform on the
sphere) ⊗ ϖ, of total mass k_d(0+). When that is finite, ρ is the Lévy measure of a compound
Poisson process of rate k_d(0+) and jump law R·U, the symmetric ρ gives β = 0 in (17.15), and
ψ(z) = −k_d(0+)(1 − φ(|z|)), so (17.12) is e^{−F} with F as in § 1. The identification is right.

*Caution, not an error.* Sato's c is the rate of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, fixed at will; the
record's and the paper's c is k_d(0+). The record uses both letters in one paragraph without
saying so. Any sentence that goes into the paper should cite Theorem 17.5 "with c = 1" or avoid
the letter.

*What (i) is for.* The identity of § 1 is proved from the paper's definitions by Tonelli alone.
Theorem 17.5 is the attribution of the map, not a premise of it: no new cited fact is needed for
the map.

**(ii) HOLDS**, as the record states it (0 < c < ∞). *Into:* for c > 0 and φ ∈ Φ_d° with radius law
σ, k_d := c·σ((·,∞)) is nonincreasing with k_d(0+) = c, the two integrability conditions hold by
the dictionary of § 1, so (0, k_d) is an isotropic origin datum, its exponent is F_φ(|·|) by § 1,
and F_φ ∈ 𝒜_d by Proposition 3.10(1)–(2). *Onto:* an F ∈ 𝒜_d with Gaussian coefficient 0 and
0 < k_d(0+) < ∞ has a datum (Proposition 3.10(1)), and § 1 writes it as F_φ. *One to one:* F is
continuously differentiable on (0,∞) with rF′(r) = c(1 − φ(r)); c is determined by F (Corollary
3.13(2), or the Cesàro mean below), hence so is φ, and φ determines σ. F ≡ 0 is the excluded case
c = 0.

## 3. (iii), both directions

**Claim.** Let c > 0 and φ ∈ Φ_d°. Then F_φ ∈ 𝒜_{d+1} if and only if φ ∈ Φ_{d+1}.

**HOLDS.** The record's proof is correct. Written out with every hypothesis:

*If.* Let φ(|·|) be the characteristic function of a law on ℝ^{d+1}; the law is rotation invariant
(its transform is), so it is the law of R′·U′, U′ uniform on S^d. Its image under the projection on
the first d coordinates has the transform φ(|·|) on ℝ^d, so it is the law of R·U of § 1, and
R has the law of R′·|U′_{1:d}|, where |U′_{1:d}|² has the Beta(d/2, 1/2) law. Two things are needed
for φ ∈ Φ_{d+1}°. No atom at 0: P(R = 0) = P(R′ = 0), since P(U′_{1:d} = 0) = P(U′ = ±e_{d+1}) = 0.
Logarithmic moment: log⁺R′ ≤ log⁺R + log(1/|U′_{1:d}|), and E log(1/|U′_{1:d}|) < ∞ (or, without
any computation, the last paragraph of § 1: the finiteness of F_φ does not know the dimension).
Then (ii) in dimension d+1 gives F_φ ∈ 𝒜_{d+1}.

*Only if.* Let F_φ ∈ 𝒜_{d+1}. By Proposition 3.10(1) in dimension d+1 it is the exponent of a datum
(a′, k′). Corollary 3.13(3), which is machine-checked, gives a′ = 0 and k′(0+) = c, because the
datum (0, c·σ((·,∞))) in dimension d has the same radial exponent. (The record proves both
directly: rF_φ′(r) ≤ 2c gives F_φ = O(log r), so a′ = 0; and F_φ(r) ≥ H_{d+1}(rδ)·ϖ′((δ,∞)) with
H_{d+1} nondecreasing and ~ log gives ϖ′((δ,∞)) ≤ 2c for every δ. Both steps are right.) So (ii) in
dimension d+1 writes F_φ = c′∫_0^r (1 − φ′(u)) du/u with φ′ ∈ Φ_{d+1}° and c′ = k′(0+). Both
integrands are continuous, so differentiating gives c(1 − φ(r)) = c′(1 − φ′(r)) for every r > 0.
With c′ = c this is φ = φ′. (The record gets c′ = c from the Cesàro means R^{−1}∫_0^R φ → σ({0}) = 0
and likewise for φ′; this is right too: for d = 1 the mean of cos(us) is sin(Rs)/(Rs), and for
d ≥ 2, Ω_d → 0.) Hence φ = φ′ ∈ Φ_{d+1}.

*Is there a gap between "φ is positive definite as a radial function on ℝ^{d+1}" and "φ is the
function produced by the (d+1)-dimensional datum"?* No. In the "if" direction a member of Φ_{d+1}
is, by Bochner's theorem, the transform of a rotation-invariant law on ℝ^{d+1}, and the only extra
facts needed are the two side conditions, which pass upward as shown. In the "only if" direction
the three uniqueness statements used are the paper's own: of the datum (Proposition 3.10(1),
which rests on C3(ii)), of a and c across dimensions (Corollary 3.13(3)), and of a continuous
function from its integral. The statement is about φ ∈ Φ_d°; for a φ with an atom at 0 it holds
after removing the atom.

**What the failure is, and is not.** In every case computed, F_φ(|ξ|) on ℝ^{d+1} is still the
exponent of an *infinitely divisible* law; what fails is the monotonicity of its polar profile,
that is self-decomposability. This matters for (vi): the paper's sentence argues about whether a
function can be of the form e^{−F}, and that is not where the dimension acts.

### 3.1 The numerical checks asked for (item 5)

A formula used below and in block F: if a rotation-invariant Lévy measure on ℝ^{D+2} has the polar
profile k_{D+2}, its shadow on ℝ^D has the polar profile k_D(r) = D·r^D∫_r^∞ k_{D+2}(s) s^{−D−1} ds
(the first D coordinates of a uniform point of S^{D+1} have a radius with distribution function
t^D), so

**k_{D+2} = k_D − (r/D)·k_D′.**

It reproduces from each other the Matérn profiles that (3.4) gives, for D = 1, …, 4 (block F). The
one-dimension step is the Abel-type shadow formula of `item09_projection_of_polar_profile.py`,
used here as a cross-check.

**Negative case, d = 2, φ = Ω_2 = J_0** (the step profile 1_{(0,1)} in the plane, not used in the
record). F_φ(r) = ∫_0^r (1 − J_0(u)) du/u. Its folded profile on the line is
k_1(x) = (2/π)·arccos x on (0,1) (the arcsine law is the shadow of the circle). The profile that
dimension 3 would need is, by the formula with D = 1,

k_3(r) = k_1 − r·k_1′ = (2/π)·(arccos r + r/√(1−r²)) on (0,1), 0 beyond.

Block B confirms, to 13 digits at ρ = 0.5, 2, 7, 20, that the line form with k_1 and the ℝ³ form
∫(1 − sin(ρr)/(ρr)) k_3(r) dr/r both equal F_φ(ρ), and that the shadow of k_3 in the plane is the
step. So k_3 is the polar profile of F_φ(|·|) on ℝ³, unique by C3(ii). It is nonnegative and
**strictly increasing** on (0,1) (k_3′ = (2/π)·r²(1−r²)^{−3/2}), unbounded at 1. By the paper's own
criterion (Proposition 3.10(1), as in the exclusion step of Theorem 3.19(1)) F_φ ∉ 𝒜_3: the
profile is not nonincreasing. It is not negative: F_φ(|ξ|) is the exponent of an infinitely
divisible law on ℝ³ that is not self-decomposable. On the φ side, J_0 ∉ Φ_3: four points at mutual
distance j_{1,1} = 3.8317 exist in ℝ³ and give the matrix eigenvalue 1 + 3J_0(j_{1,1}) = −0.208.

**Positive case, φ = Ω_3 = sin u/u.** F_φ(r) = ∫_0^r (1 − sin u/u) du/u = Cin(r) + sin r/r − 1.
Block C: its profiles are k_3 = 1_{(0,1)}, k_2 = √(1−r²) on (0,1), k_1 = (1−x)_+, each reproducing
F_φ to 13 digits and each nonincreasing, so F_φ ∈ 𝒜_1, 𝒜_2, 𝒜_3, as Ω_3 ∈ Φ_1, Φ_2, Φ_3. Dimension
4 would need k_4 = k_2 − (r/2)k_2′ = (2−r²)/(2√(1−r²)), which reproduces F_φ and increases
(k_4′ = r³(1−r²)^{−3/2}/2): F_φ ∉ 𝒜_4, as Ω_3 ∉ Φ_4.

**From the definition, with no map and no profile.** F ∈ 𝒜_D requires, for every λ ∈ (0,1), that
the residual ξ ↦ exp(−(F(|ξ|) − F(λ|ξ|))) be a characteristic function on ℝ^D.

- `c_filtration_residual_pd.py` searches point sets for a negative eigenvalue of the residual
  matrix at λ = 0.9. For φ = J_0 it finds none in the plane and a nine-point set in ℝ³ with
  smallest eigenvalue **−0.075** (confirmed with F by direct quadrature). For φ = cos, the Cin
  ray, none on the line and a nine-point set in the plane with **−0.374**. For φ = sin u/u none in
  ℝ², ℝ³, and none in ℝ⁴; for the hat h_1 none on the line and none in the plane. A search that
  finds nothing decides nothing, so the last two exclusions were tested otherwise:
- `c_filtration_bochner_test.py` integrates the residual against the transform of a nonnegative
  radial shell W(x) ∝ |x|^{2p}e^{−a|x|²}. If the residual were the transform of a law ρ the result
  would be ∫W dρ ≥ 0. For φ = sin u/u in ℝ⁴ it is **negative** (−0.0098 at λ = 0.9, −0.0025 at
  λ = 0.97) and positive in ℝ³ with the same shells. For Gneiting's h_1 in the plane, with the
  shell placed where the profile k_2(s) = s^{−1}∫_0^s J_0 increases (5.85 < s < 8.0 at λ = 0.98),
  it is **negative** (−8.9·10⁻⁶ and −9.8·10⁻⁶ for two shells) and positive on the line. In all six
  negative results the value agrees within 2.3 % with the first-order prediction from the profile,
  λ^c∫W(s)(k_D(s) − k_D(s/λ)) ds/s. So the profile computation and the definition agree
  quantitatively, not only in sign.

So (iii) is confirmed on J_0 (2 → 3), sin u/u (3 → 4), cos (1 → 2) and h_1 (1 → 2), in each case
by the profile and by the definition.

## 4. (iv): the classical strictness and its witnesses

**The corollary HOLDS:** every φ ∈ Φ_d° ∖ Φ_{d+1} gives F_φ ∈ 𝒜_d ∖ 𝒜_{d+1}, by (ii) and (iii).

**Gneiting's hat, HOLDS, and it is the printed witness.** Read at the page image of p. 94: *"an
n-dimensional random vector with characteristic function h_n(‖x‖) is absolutely continuous with
probability density function p_n(x) = … = Γ(n/2+1)/π^{n/2} · J²_{n/2}(‖x‖/2)/‖x‖^n (15) … This
leads to the important fact that h_n(t) belongs to the class Φ_n, but not to Φ_{n+1} or any higher
class."* The argument continues on p. 95 through the marginal density (16), cited there to Fang,
Kotz and Ng, Theorem 2.10. The side conditions: the law has a density, so no atom; its radial
density is a constant times J²_{n/2}(r/2)/r = O(r^{−2}), so the logarithmic moment is finite (or:
1 − h_n(u) = O(u) at 0, and § 1). So F(r) = c∫_0^r (1 − h_d(u)) du/u ∈ 𝒜_d ∖ 𝒜_{d+1} for every
d ≥ 1. The record's closed form at d = 1 (r for r ≤ 1, 1 + log r beyond) is right, and § 3.1
confirms the exclusion from 𝒜_2 from the definition.

**Schoenberg's Ω_d, the correction.** The record writes: every member of Φ_{d+1} is bounded below
by inf Ω_{d+1} (Schoenberg's Theorem 1), "and inf Ω_d < inf Ω_{d+1} (−1, −0.4028, −0.2172, −0.1323,
…; block F)". Three things:

1. *The inequality is computed for four values and asserted for all.* It is true for every d, and
   the proof is two lines. Ω_{d+1}(t) = ∫_0^1 Ω_d(ts) β_d(ds), where β_d is the law of |U′_{1:d}|,
   with density 2s^{d−1}(1−s²)^{−1/2}/B(d/2, 1/2) (this is Φ_{d+1} ⊂ Φ_d applied to Ω_{d+1}; block D
   checks it for d = 1, …, 4). Ω_{d+1} is negative somewhere and tends to 0, so it attains its
   infimum at a finite t*; and Ω_d(t*s) > inf Ω_d for almost every s, Ω_d being analytic and not
   constant. So inf Ω_{d+1} = Ω_{d+1}(t*) > inf Ω_d. Block D lists the infima for d = 1, …, 10
   (−1, −0.40276, −0.21723, −0.13228, −0.08617, …), strictly increasing.
2. *It is not printed where the record points.* Schoenberg's p. 816, at the image, has Theorem 1
   and the chain 𝔓(E_1) ⊃ 𝔓(E_2) ⊃ … with ⊃ meaning inclusion, and no statement of strictness
   (`READING-schoenberg1938metric.md` § 5 says the same). So "the strictness of Schoenberg's chain"
   in the record's conclusion is, as a printed statement, Gneiting's p. 94; that Ω_d itself
   separates is the record's own inference, correct, with the proof above.
3. *A shorter proof, needing no infimum.* The projection to ℝ^d of a rotation-invariant law on
   ℝ^{d+1} is P(R′ = 0)·δ_0 plus an absolutely continuous measure, because the first d coordinates
   of a uniform point of S^d have a density on the unit ball. The uniform law on a sphere of ℝ^d
   is singular and has no atom at 0. So Ω_d(τ·) ∉ Φ_{d+1}, and more generally no φ ∈ Φ_d° whose
   radius law has a singular part is in Φ_{d+1}.

*The step profile:* σ = δ_τ gives k_d = c·σ((r,∞)) = c·1_{(0,τ)}(r). Confirmed, and numerically in
block A1 for d = 1, …, 4. So the exponent of the datum (0, c·1_{(0,τ)}) in dimension d, which the
paper displays after Proposition 3.10 as an example of a member of 𝒜_d, is in 𝒜_d ∖ 𝒜_{d+1}.

**"A few lines from printed statements the paper already cites?"** Yes to the first half, no to
the second.

- The lines: (ii) is Tonelli and Proposition 3.10; (iii) adds Corollary 3.13(3); then one witness.
  With Gneiting's witness the one outside statement is his p. 94. With the step profile nothing

- The paper's cited facts are C1–C7. C6 is Schoenberg's theorem on the functions negative definite

  Gneiting 1999 and Schoenberg 1938 are in the bibliography as prior work and carry no statement.
  Sato's Theorem 17.5 is not cited at all at present. So the short proof through Gneiting would add

- **The paper already contains the step-profile proof.** The proof of Theorem 3.20 in Appendix A

## 5. (v): the Cin ray

**HOLDS.** The paper's definition (§ 2.3, after Proposition 2.7; Theorem 3.20): a member of a Cin
ray is F(ω) = b·Cin(τ|ω|), b > 0, τ > 0, the admissible exponent with Gaussian coefficient 0 and
folded profile b·1_{(0,τ)}, where Cin(z) = ∫_0^z (1 − cos v) dv/v. In the dictionary of § 1 at
d = 1: c = b, σ = δ_τ, φ(u) = cos(τu) = Ω_1(τu) ∈ Φ_1°, and F_φ(r) = b∫_0^r (1 − cos τu) du/u =
b·Cin(τr). By (iii) at d = 1, F ∈ 𝒜_2 if and only if cos(τ·) ∈ Φ_2. It is not: by Schoenberg's
Theorem 1 every member of Φ_2 is a mixture of the functions J_0(tu), and J_0(t) =
π^{−1}∫_0^π cos(t sin θ) dθ > −1 for every t, while cos(π) = −1 (no numerical infimum is needed; or
§ 4, point 3, with d = 1). The nesting 𝒜_d ⊆ 𝒜_2 gives every d ≥ 2. So Theorem 3.20 is exactly
the case d = 1 of the Ω_d witness, as the record says.

Block E adds what the plane and ℝ³ make of the ray: Cin(|ξ|) is the exponent of the Lévy measure
with polar profile (1−s²)^{−1/2} on the unit disc of the plane, and of the measure 1_{(0,1)} dr/r +
δ_1 in radius on ℝ³ (both reproduce Cin to 14 digits). Both are nonnegative. So the ray stays
infinitely divisible in the plane and in space and loses self-decomposability, and the residual at
λ = 0.9 fails to be positive definite on a nine-point set of the plane (§ 3.1).

## 6. Is the correspondence in print, and does it matter (item 6)

**The representation itself is in print in two held sources**, for every self-decomposable law:
Sato's Theorem 17.5 (§ 2), and Jurek–Mason, read at the images of pp. 127–128: the mapping
(3.6.17) 𝒥_Q(μ) := 𝓛(∫_0^∞ e^{−tQ} dY(t)) from ID_log, "the set of all infinitely divisible
measures μ with ∫log(1 + ‖x‖)μ(dx) < ∞", onto L_0(Q); Remark 3.6.9(4), *"A measure μ is in L_0(Q) if
and only if there exists a unique measure ν ∈ ID_log such that μ̂(y) = exp∫_0^∞ log ν̂(e^{−tQ*}y)
dt"*; and **Proposition 3.6.10**: *"(a) The mapping 𝒥_Q is an algebraic isomorphism between the
semigroups L_0(Q) and ID_log. … (c) If the linear operator T commutes with Q, then 𝒥_Q(Tμ) =
T𝒥_Q(μ) for μ ∈ ID_log."* At Q = I, where L_0(I) is the class of the self-decomposable laws (their
(3.6.1), seen in the text layer only), every linear T commutes with Q. So clause (c) with T a
rotation, and with T the orthogonal projection of ℝ^{d+1} onto ℝ^d × {0}, together with the
injectivity in (a), gives the general form of (ii) and (iii) at once, with no restriction to a
finite c or to a zero Gaussian part:

> F ∈ 𝒜_d if and only if ψ(r) := rF′(r) is the radial exponent of a rotation-invariant infinitely
> divisible law on ℝ^d with a finite logarithmic moment; and the driver of the projection is the
> projection of the driver.

The record under check does not cite Jurek–Mason; it should, since clause (c) is the printed form
of "the map carries the inclusions". I have not proved the general form here beyond the finite-c
case; block H checks it on the paper's own family, which has c = ∞.

**Whether the specialisation to radial laws, or the chain 𝒜_d under any name, is printed, I could
not determine.** The record found no source that names the classes; I found none either. A web
search for orientation (two engines, three queries each) returned nothing usable: unrelated hits
or none. From recollection only, and not as a citation: the representation goes back to Wolfe
(1982), Jurek and Vervaat (1983) and Sato and Yamazato (1984).

**Does it matter?** For what the paper may claim, the question "is the statement
𝒜_d ⊋ 𝒜_{d+1} printed?" is the wrong one. The statement is a corollary, in a few lines, of two
printed results the paper would have to cite side by side: the isomorphism with its commutation
rule (Jurek–Mason, Proposition 3.6.10(a), (c); or Sato's Theorem 17.5) and the strictness of the
radial positive definite chain (Gneiting, p. 94). A referee who sees both will not accept "new"
for it, printed or not, and the present text gives a reason why the second result is irrelevant
that is false. The paper can say that it has not found the statement in print and that it follows
from these two; it cannot list it as a contribution.

Under the map the family has a plain description (block H): the driver of F^(w) has the exponent

ψ_w(r) = rF^(w)′(r) = √(2π)·r + 2w·(e^{−r²/2} − e^{−r²}),

a Cauchy exponent plus w times a difference of two Gaussian transforms, and its Lévy density on
ℝ^d is √(2π)·Γ(m)π^{−m}|x|^{−d−1} + 2w(g_2(x) − g_1(x)), g_u the centred Gaussian density of covariance
uI. This is nonnegative exactly when w·z^m(e^{−z} − 2^{−d/2}e^{−z/2}) ≤ Γ(m)/2 at z = |x|²/2, which is
the paper's criterion (A.11), `eq:threshold-criterion` (the cone paper's (A.2)), to the letter. So **w_d is the largest w for which the Cauchy Lévy
density dominates the Gaussian difference in ℝ^d**, and Theorem 3.19(1) says that ψ_w is a Lévy
exponent on ℝ^d and not on ℝ^{d+1}. Block H confirms it for d = 1, …, 8: w_1 = 12.606,
w_2 = 5.2423, w_3 = 3.2056 (the paper's 12.61, 5.24, 3.21), the density changing sign at the
critical radius between 0.999·w_d and 1.001·w_d, and the radial measure of the driver equal to
−dk_d^(w) of (A.10), `eq:witness-polar`. The map restates the paper's computation; it does not supply it. The record's
"none of that follows from the map" is right.

**One qualitative thing the family gives and the classical witnesses do not.** Every F^(w) is a
Bernstein function of r²/2 (its delay profile is nonnegative), so e^{−F^(w)(|ξ|)} is a Gaussian
variance mixture and the transform of an infinitely divisible law in *every* dimension (the
exclusion step of the paper's proof constructs that law). The separation of 𝒜_d from 𝒜_{d+1}
therefore already takes place inside the exponents that are infinitely divisible in every
dimension, Schoenberg's limit class, and what distinguishes dimension d+1 is self-decomposability
alone. The classical witnesses are not of this kind: Cin(√(2σ)) has the derivative
(1 − cos√(2σ))/(2σ), which vanishes at isolated points without vanishing identically, so it is not
completely monotone and Cin(|ξ|) is not infinitely divisible in every dimension (it is in the
plane and in ℝ³, § 5). The paper has the ingredients of this statement in its lead to § 3.4 ("built
like a subordinated exponent, and the witnesses are not subordinated") and does not state it as a
result. Also: the family has c = ∞, outside the range of the finite-c map of the record; the
general form of § 6 covers it.

### Theorem 3.20 (`thm:no-cin-ray-in-the-plane`)

The whole statement follows from the map and classical facts: (iii) at d = 1 with φ = cos(τ·),
"cos is not a mixture of dilates of J_0", and the nesting. No clause is left over. What remains
the paper's is the reading (the extreme rays of the cone of the line, other than the Gaussian, are
lost at the first step: under the map this is the classical fact that the two-point law
(δ_τ + δ_{−τ})/2 is not the shadow of a rotation-invariant law of the plane), a self-contained
proof on C2–C4, and its formalisation. The same proof gives the statement in every dimension
(§ 4): no datum with a step profile in dimension d is in 𝒜_{d+1}, which by Schoenberg's Theorem 1
is the statement that the extreme members of each class are lost at the next step.
